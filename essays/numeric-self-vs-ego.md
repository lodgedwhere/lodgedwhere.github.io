**A Minimal Model of Self as Unrepresentable Constraint**

Consider [a minimal system consisting of three bits](essays/minimal-model-of-ego.md): two bits of internal memory and one stochastic input bit. At each step, the system updates its state by applying one of two simple logical rules (e.g., AND or OR with a negated input), selected randomly by the external bit. The result is a recursively generated bitstream with nontrivial statistical structure. Despite the randomness of the input and the absence of any semantic content, the system exhibits stable mutual information between its internal (2 bit memory) state and the external signal.

This construction reveals a basic structural fact: meaningful statistical dependence can arise purely from internal constraints acting on stochastic input. No reference to an external world model or symbolic representation is required. The system does not “understand” anything, yet it produces a structured coupling between its internal state and its input.

A crucial feature of this model is that the rules governing state evolution (the logic elements and pathways) are not representable within the system’s 3-bit instantaneous state. At any given moment, only the current bits are present; the law that transforms them is not itself encoded as part of that state. One can expand the 3-bit ontology—e.g., by passing to a Markov representation in which four states encode the possibilities of the 2-bit memory—but this merely internalizes previously external structure at the cost of increasing the descriptive complexity. At the new level, a new form of “law” again appears that is not fully representable within that ontology. Thus, no fixed level of description can be both closed and fully self-grounding.

This suggests a minimal and non-anthropomorphic distinction between “ego” and “self.” The ego is the observable, representable structure: the evolving bitstream together with its induced correlations and memory. It is the part of the system that can be described within the ontology at a given level. In contrast, the self corresponds to the source of constraint (here, the logic circuit) that shapes this evolution but is not itself an object within the system’s representational frame. It is not directly accessible as a state, but only inferred through its effects on the dynamics of the ego.

From this perspective, what might be called “experience” is not a property of isolated states, but of the ongoing coupling between internal dynamics and uncontrolled external input. The system’s internal state does not mirror the world, nor does it predict it in a strong sense when the input is purely stochastic. Instead, it maintains a persistent statistical dependence on the input—a structured relationship induced by its own constraints.

The model therefore supports a minimal structural claim: any system that generates nontrivial internal structure from stochastic input must rely on constraints that are not fully representable within its current ontology. Identifying this unrepresentable source of constraint with “I” yields a stripped-down notion of self—one grounded not in representation or meaning, but in the unavoidable existence of a boundary between what is expressed and what makes expression possible.

This boundary cannot be eliminated, only shifted. Each attempt to internalize the governing structure expands the ontology and produces a new level at which the same distinction reappears. In this sense, the self is not a fixed entity, but a structural feature of any recursively constrained process: the always-present, never-fully-representable condition of its own evolution.
