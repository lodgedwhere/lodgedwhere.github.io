**“Wave”, “Particle”, and the Limits of Description**

A persistent tension runs through our most refined descriptions of reality: no single level of description can be both fully closed—self-contained and dynamically complete—and fully self-grounding—capable of producing definite, individuated facts from within itself. This is not a defect of particular theories, but a structural feature that reappears across domains, most clearly in physics and mathematics.

In quantum physics, this tension appears as wave–particle duality. The particle description offers self-grounding: it presents definite events, localized outcomes, and discrete facts. A detector clicks here, not there. But this description is not dynamically closed; it cannot account, within itself, for the continuous processes that give rise to those events. The wave description offers the opposite: a closed dynamical evolution governed by equations that are complete and internally consistent. Yet it does not ground the definiteness of outcomes. It describes a spread of possibilities without explaining why one actualizes.

Modern formulations such as Quantum Field Theory shift the perspective. The ontology is unified: fields evolve continuously, and particles become excitations of those fields. This appears to reconcile the duality. But the reconciliation is asymmetric. The formalism emphasizes closure—continuous, unitary evolution—while the grounding of definite outcomes is displaced to the boundary: measurement, asymptotic states, or observational context. What was once explicit in the particle picture becomes implicit, hidden in how the theory is connected to experience. The tension is not resolved; it is repositioned.

The same structure appears in mathematics in the contrast between local and global representations of functions. In the basis of Dirac Delta Function, a function is reconstructed pointwise:  
  
$f(x) = \int f(x')\delta(x - x')dx'$.  
  
Here, each basis element is maximally self-grounding: it isolates a definite value at a point. But the representation is not closed in any finite or well-behaved sense; it requires an uncountable family of distributions that lie outside ordinary function space.

In the Fourier Transform representation, the situation is reversed:  
  
$f(x) = \int \tilde{f}(k)e^{ikx}dk$.  
  
This expansion is globally closed: it provides a complete, internally coherent structure in which operations like differentiation and evolution become simple and self-contained. Yet no individual component is self-grounding. The plane waves are non-normalizable and delocalized; no single element corresponds to a definite value or event. Localization emerges only through the coordinated superposition of infinitely many modes.

In both cases, completeness requires the coexistence of mutually incomplete perspectives. One may adopt a representation that grounds individual elements or one that closes the system under its own operations, but not both simultaneously at the same level. Attempts at unification—whether by moving to field ontology in physics or to more abstract functional spaces in mathematics—do not eliminate the tension. They privilege one side, internalizing it within the formalism, while the other recedes to a boundary condition, a limiting procedure, or an interpretive step.

This suggests a general principle: any representational framework must distinguish between what is described and what makes description determinate. Closure and self-grounding cannot coincide without remainder. Where one is made explicit, the other becomes implicit.

Thus, the lesson of wave–particle duality and its mathematical analogues is not merely that reality admits multiple descriptions, but that no single description can be both complete and foundational at once. The unity we seek is not the elimination of this tension, but its recognition as intrinsic to the act of representation itself.
