**Cannabis as Keisaku**

In Zen monasteries, the keisaku is a flat wooden stick carried by the monitor during meditation. To an outsider it appears punitive, but its purpose is not punishment. It is a reminder. A sharp interruption. A sudden return to presence when the mind has drifted into sleep, fantasy, or compulsive thought. The blow does not create awareness; it merely recalls it. One wakes not because of the stick, but because the distraction momentarily collapses.

Cannabis, at very small doses, can function in precisely this way.

Not as revelation. Not as escape. Not as entertainment. Not even as enhancement. Rather, as a brief disruption of the continuity of habitual mental grasping. A tap on the shoulder from within the machinery of thought itself.

The modern attitude toward cannabis almost always mistakes quantity for depth. More intoxication is assumed to mean more insight, as though truth were hidden behind progressively stronger distortions of consciousness. But direct observation suggests the opposite. The clarity appears early, quietly, and then quickly becomes obscured beneath increasing cognitive noise. Beyond a certain point, additional dosage does not deepen presence; it merely elaborates experience. Thought becomes fragmented, memory unstable, associations proliferate, and the mind becomes fascinated with its own reflections.

The essential insight is already present before all of that.

At low doses, one may notice something remarkably simple: the continuity of the self depends upon constant memory rehearsal. Identity is not found as a stable object anywhere in experience. It is actively reconstructed from moment to moment through recollection, anticipation, comparison, narration, and internal repetition. The ordinary mind performs this reconstruction so continuously that its activity becomes invisible, like the hum of electrical equipment in a familiar room.

Cannabis slightly interferes with this rehearsal process. Short-term memory loosens. The compulsive tendency to maintain narrative continuity weakens. And in the gap produced by this interruption, something obvious becomes visible: experiencing itself was never divided in the first place. The separate self was being continually asserted into existence through thought.

For many people this disruption is unpleasant. Anxiety appears immediately. They grasp harder at identity, trying to restore continuity and control. Others experience relief, though they often misinterpret the relief as pleasure produced by the substance itself. But perhaps the relief comes not from addition, but subtraction—from the temporary suspension of the burden of maintaining a psychological self-image.

In this sense, cannabis resembles the keisaku. It does not transmit truth. It does not contain wisdom. It merely interrupts the trance long enough for one to notice what had always been present beneath it.

And this explains why more is unnecessary.

The keisaku is not improved by striking continuously. Once the meditator awakens, further blows become counterproductive. Likewise, once presence has been recalled, increasing intoxication adds nothing essential. In fact, it often obscures the very clarity that first appeared. The reminder becomes another distraction. The pointer becomes another object of attachment.

This distinction is critical because human beings habitually transform every pointer into a possession. Teachings become doctrines. Meditation becomes achievement. Insight becomes identity. Even ego dissolution becomes a new egoic performance. The mind endlessly converts reminders into structures of psychological ownership.

But the deepest spiritual traditions consistently point toward something simpler: truth is not acquired. It is remembered—not as memory of past information, but as recognition of what has never once been absent.

Nothing external can manufacture this recognition. At most, something may interrupt the mechanisms obscuring it.

A bell.
A silence.
A breath.
A shock.
A line of poetry.
A glimpse of mortality.
A small dose of cannabis.

*Keisaku.*
