# Meaning, aboutness, reentry

Meaning appears so natural that it is rarely questioned. A word is about an object. A thought is about a feeling. A proposition is about another proposition. The relation seems obvious. Yet upon inspection, “aboutness” is difficult to locate. It is not contained in either object individually. It appears only in the relationship between them.

To say that one proposition is about another already requires a context within which both propositions are interpreted. The statement “A is about B” introduces a third term: the meta-level that establishes the relation. Meaning therefore seems to require a distinction between theory and metatheory, object and context.

But this immediately raises a problem. The metatheory is itself another proposition. If it explains the relation between A and B, what explains the relation between the metatheory and its own subject matter? Another metatheory must be introduced. The hierarchy extends indefinitely.

The possibility of reentry changes the picture. If a distinction can become part of its own context, then the hierarchy need not extend forever. The meta-level folds back into the object-level. Theory and metatheory become different aspects of a single operation.

Viewed in this way, aboutness may be the semantic appearance of reentry. What appears as a relation between two objects is actually a fold within one space. The distinction and the context that interprets it are not fundamentally separate.

This suggests a deeper possibility. Perhaps meaning is not primitive at all. Perhaps it is generated by the first reentrant distinction: the appearance of self and world.

The distinction “I / not-I” creates a privileged point of reference. Once established, every subsequent distinction inherits its structure. Objects become things-for-a-subject. Thoughts become thoughts-about-objects. Propositions become propositions-about-propositions. The entire architecture of meaning unfolds from the original asymmetry between observer and observed.

Yet the observer and the observed are themselves products of the same distinction. The asymmetry is not contained in either side. It arises from the act of distinction itself. Meaning is therefore not a property of things. It is the shadow cast by a hidden distinction that cannot be directly observed from within the space it generates.

This perspective casts new light on the biblical phrase, “I am that I am.”  
Ordinary statements derive meaning by referring beyond themselves. A subject is interpreted through a predicate. An object is explained by a context. But “I am that I am” offers no external context. The thing being explained and the explanation are identical.  
The statement does not point outside itself for grounding. It contains its own context.

In structural terms, the metatheory has folded completely into the theory. The distinction between content and context collapses. The statement ceases to be about anything beyond itself.

This is why it sounds almost empty to the analytical mind. No new information is conveyed. Yet perhaps that is precisely its function. It does not provide another description. It refuses the search for a larger framework within which the speaker can be understood.

The statement stands in its own context.  

If meaning arises from the fold between object and context, then “I am that I am” points to the limit where that fold no longer generates an apparent inside and outside. 

Nothing is explained by anything else.   
Nothing refers beyond itself. What remains is not a proposition about reality, but reality prior to aboutness itself.  
It simply is.
