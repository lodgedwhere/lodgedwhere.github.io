# The Breath of Nature

When we look at a plant, we often see an object: a thing with roots, leaves, and flowers. But if we look more carefully, the plant begins to dissolve into processes.  
It is the carbon cycle taking form. It is water drawn upward from the soil and returned to the sky. It is sunlight transformed into sugar and structure. It is minerals lifted from stone and woven into living tissue. What we call “the plant” is a temporary meeting place of countless flows.

The plant germinates, grows, flowers, and dies. Yet none of these events belong to the plant alone. They are movements of the Earth itself. Nature inhales, gathering matter and energy into form. Nature exhales, releasing that form back into the larger circulation from which it arose.

The same is true of every organism.  
A deer is not separate from the forest. A bird is not separate from the sky. A human is not separate from the world that sustains them. Each is a pattern through which larger processes are expressing themselves. The carbon in our bodies, the water in our blood, the oxygen in our lungs, and the energy fueling our thoughts all arrive from elsewhere and eventually return.

We are not independent entities inhabiting nature. We are nature in one of its innumerable forms.

This perspective reveals something surprising about individuality. The forms differ endlessly. No two trees are the same. No two people share the same history. Every life unfolds according to its own unique pattern.

Yet the process itself is identical.  
Breathing is just breathing.  
The inhalation that becomes an oak tree and the inhalation that becomes a human being are different expressions of the same movement. The exhalation that returns them to the world is the same release. Birth and death, growth and decay, appearance and disappearance are not separate events happening to separate things. They are phases of a single unfolding.

The wave may imagine itself distinct from every other wave, but all waves are movements of the same water. Likewise, each individual life is a unique gesture, yet the life expressing itself through that gesture is not divided by the form it takes.

Seen this way, what is most deeply shared among beings is not any particular characteristic. It is not intelligence, language, culture, or biology. It is the simple fact of appearing at all.

Before human and plant,  
before self and other,  
before birth and death,  
there is the movement itself.  
An endless breathing.  
Nature gathering itself into form and releasing itself again.  
The shapes of the breath change endlessly, but the breathing never ceases.
